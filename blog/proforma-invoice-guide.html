Proforma Invoice Guide

By QueckBiz Team·June 11, 2026·8 min read

Proforma invoices are frequently misunderstood business documents. They look like regular invoices but serve a completely different purpose. Used correctly, proforma invoices help businesses quote prices, arrange pre-payments, and streamline international trade. This guide explains everything you need to know about creating and using proforma invoices effectively.

What Is a Proforma Invoice?

A proforma invoice is a preliminary bill of sale sent to buyers before a transaction is finalized. It is not a true invoice because it does not demand payment. Instead, it provides a detailed quote that the buyer can use to arrange payment, obtain import permits, or secure financing.

The term proforma means for the sake of form. It describes a document that has the form of an invoice but not the legal obligation. Think of it as a commitment to sell at specified terms rather than a demand for payment.

When to Use a Proforma Invoice

Proforma invoices are commonly used in international trade where buyers need documentation to arrange letters of credit or import licenses. They are used when requesting advance payment or deposits before work begins. They also serve as formal quotations for large or complex orders.

If a customer requests a detailed price breakdown before committing to purchase, a proforma invoice provides that information in a familiar format. It gives buyers the documentation they need while protecting sellers from premature payment obligations.

Proforma Invoice vs. Commercial Invoice

The key difference is legal status. A commercial invoice is a demand for payment that creates an accounts receivable entry. A proforma invoice is informational and does not create a payment obligation. You cannot use a proforma invoice to collect payment.

Commercial invoices are issued after goods are shipped or services are delivered. Proforma invoices are issued before the transaction is complete. Once the buyer accepts the proforma terms and the transaction proceeds, a commercial invoice replaces the proforma for payment purposes.

What to Include on a Proforma Invoice

A proforma invoice should include the same information as a commercial invoice. Start with your business details and the buyer's information. Include a unique proforma number and the date of issue. Clearly label the document as PROFORMA INVOICE to avoid confusion.

Itemize the products or services with descriptions, quantities, unit prices, and line totals. Include the subtotal, taxes, shipping costs, and grand total. Specify payment terms, delivery terms, and the validity period of the offer. Many proforma invoices include a note stating that this is not a final tax invoice.

Validity Periods and Expiration

Proforma invoices should include an expiration date. Prices, availability, and exchange rates can change over time. A standard validity period is 30 days, though this varies by industry. After expiration, the proforma is no longer binding and a new one may be needed.

Clearly state the validity period on the document. This protects you from being held to outdated prices and encourages buyers to make decisions promptly. If you extend a proforma, issue a revised version with a new date and validity period.

Proforma Invoices in International Trade

International trade relies heavily on proforma invoices. Buyers use them to apply for import licenses, arrange foreign exchange, and open letters of credit. Customs authorities may review proforma invoices to assess duties before goods arrive.

For international proforma invoices, include Incoterms that specify who is responsible for shipping, insurance, and customs clearance. Common terms include FOB, CIF, and DDP. These terms prevent disputes about shipping responsibilities and costs.

Using Proforma Invoices for Deposits

When requesting advance payment, a proforma invoice formalizes the request. The buyer can use it to justify the payment to their accounting department. Once the deposit is received, you proceed with the work and issue a commercial invoice for the balance.

Clearly indicate on the proforma what portion is required as deposit and what the balance will be. This transparency prevents confusion about payment expectations. Some businesses issue separate proforma invoices for deposits and final payments.

Legal Considerations

While proforma invoices are not legally binding as payment demands, they can create contractual obligations if accepted by the buyer. In some jurisdictions, a proforma invoice that is accepted and acted upon may constitute a contract. Include language that clarifies the document's preliminary nature.

Never use proforma invoices to evade tax reporting. Tax authorities understand the difference between proforma and commercial invoices, but they will scrutinize businesses that misuse proforma documents to delay revenue recognition improperly.

Best Practices for Proforma Invoices

Always clearly label the document as proforma. Use a numbering system separate from your commercial invoices. Include all relevant terms and conditions. Set reasonable validity periods. Follow up promptly when proforma invoices expire.

Keep copies of all proforma invoices issued. Track which proforma invoices have been accepted and converted to commercial invoices. This tracking helps you manage your sales pipeline and follow up with prospects who received proforma quotes.

Conclusion

Proforma invoices are valuable tools for quoting, pre-payment arrangements, and international trade. They provide the documentation buyers need without creating premature payment obligations. By understanding when and how to use them, you improve your sales process and reduce transaction friction.

Create professional proforma invoices that include all necessary details, clearly state their preliminary nature, and set appropriate validity periods. With good proforma practices, you set clear expectations, protect your interests, and move transactions forward smoothly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a proforma invoice?

A proforma invoice is a preliminary invoice sent before goods or services are delivered. It outlines the expected cost, quantity, and terms of a transaction but is not a demand for payment and does not record a sale in your accounts receivable. It is used to give clients a clear picture of what they will be billed before the final invoice is issued.

Is a proforma invoice legally binding?

No, a proforma invoice is not legally binding. It is a good-faith estimate or preliminary agreement that both parties understand may change before the final invoice. However, it establishes mutual understanding of the expected terms and is often used as the basis for a client to arrange financing, obtain import permits, or get internal budget approval.

What is the difference between a proforma invoice and a quote?

A proforma invoice looks like an actual invoice and includes the same detailed information (line items, quantities, prices, taxes). A quote or estimate is often less formal and may present a range of options. Proforma invoices are more common in international trade and larger B2B transactions. In practice, both serve a similar purpose: informing the client of expected costs before final billing.

Proforma Invoices in International Trade

Proforma invoices play a particularly important role in international trade, where they serve purposes beyond simply informing a buyer of expected costs. Customs authorities in many countries require a proforma invoice to accompany goods during the import process, especially when the final commercial invoice is not yet available. The proforma invoice provides the information needed to calculate customs duties and determine whether an import license is required before the actual shipment occurs.

When goods are shipped internationally, the proforma invoice typically accompanies the shipment documentation alongside the bill of lading or airway bill. It identifies the goods being shipped, their declared value, their country of origin, and the terms of the sale (Incoterms such as FOB, CIF, or DDP that define who bears responsibility for shipping costs and risk at each stage of transit). Customs officials use this information to classify the goods and assess applicable duties and taxes.

For the buyer, a proforma invoice in international trade serves as the basis for applying for a letter of credit from their bank. The letter of credit is a bank guarantee that the seller will receive payment once specific conditions — typically the presentation of shipping documents — are met. Banks require the proforma invoice to issue the letter of credit, making it an essential document in international payment processes.

Differences Between Proforma and Commercial Invoices

A proforma invoice and a commercial invoice look similar — both include the seller's and buyer's information, a description of goods or services, quantities, and prices. The critical difference is their legal and accounting status. A commercial invoice is a binding demand for payment that records a completed sale; it is entered into both the seller's accounts receivable and the buyer's accounts payable. A proforma invoice is neither of these things.

From an accounting standpoint, proforma invoices do not trigger journal entries in either party's books. They are not recorded as income by the seller or as a liability by the buyer. This is precisely what makes them useful for preliminary transactions — they provide all the detail of an invoice without the accounting and legal consequences of one. When the actual transaction is complete and the commercial invoice is issued, that is when the accounting entries are made.

Always mark your proforma invoices clearly and prominently as "PROFORMA INVOICE" to prevent any confusion with a commercial invoice. Some jurisdictions have specific requirements about how proforma invoices must be labeled. A proforma invoice that could be mistaken for a commercial invoice creates accounting confusion for the recipient and potentially creates the impression that a binding sale has occurred when it has not.
